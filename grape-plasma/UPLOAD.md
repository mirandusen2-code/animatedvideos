## Description
Cut a grape almost in half (or put two grapes so they touch) in a microwave, and a ball of glowing plasma shoots out. For years people blamed the skin acting as an antenna. In 2019 physicists at Trent University showed the real reason. Microwaves are light too, just with a long wavelength: about 12 cm (4.8 inches) in a 2.45 GHz oven. Inside water that wavelength shrinks roughly 9–10 times, to about half an inch, which is the size of a grape. So a grape-sized ball of water traps the microwaves and they bounce around inside it, building up. Where two grapes touch, the two trapped fields combine into one tiny hotspot, about a millimetre across (the size of a pinhead). The field there is strong enough to rip electrons off the sodium and potassium salts in the juice, and that cloud of charged gas is plasma. The proof: skinless water beads (hydrogel, soaked in a little salt water) do exactly the same, while a single bead does nothing. Why do physicists care? Squeezing light into spots much smaller than its wavelength is what nanophotonics is about, for sharper imaging and sensing, and grapes do it for free.

⚠️ Don't try this at home: it can damage your microwave's magnetron and the plasma is extremely hot.

Sources: Khattak, Bianucci & Slepkov, "Linking plasma formation in grapes to microwave resonances of aqueous dimers", PNAS 116, 4000 (2019); Physics World, "Grape plasma phenomenon explained at long last"; Physics Today, "Fruit photonics and the shape of water"; PBS NOVA, "How does microwaving grapes create plumes of plasma?"; Science Friday interview with Aaron Slepkov.
