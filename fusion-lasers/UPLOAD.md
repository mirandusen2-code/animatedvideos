## Description
At the National Ignition Facility in California, 192 giant lasers fire at a fuel capsule smaller than a peppercorn (0.08 in), inside a gold can 0.4 in long. Why? Scientists want to build a star on Earth. Fusion is what powers the Sun: light atoms merge into helium and release huge amounts of energy, with no carbon emissions. The fuel is deuterium, which comes from ordinary seawater, and tritium, which a power plant would breed from lithium. For a split second the pellet's core gets hotter than 100 million °F, almost 4× hotter than the center of the Sun (27 million °F). In April 2025 one shot gave back 8.6 MJ from 2.1 MJ of laser light: about 4× the energy that hit the target. The catch: the lasers draw far more power from the grid than that, and they fire a few times a day, while a power plant would need about 10 shots per second. So a real fusion power plant is still years away.

Sources: Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (lasers.llnl.gov, NIF record shot April 2025); U.S. DOE / NNSA fusion pages; Physics Today, "Harnessing energy from laser fusion".
