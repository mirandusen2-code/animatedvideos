## Description
The first jet airliner, the de Havilland Comet, had squarish windows. In 1954 two Comets broke apart in mid-air within three months: BOAC Flight 781 off Elba (January 10) and South African Airways Flight 201 off Naples (April 8). Why? On every flight the cabin is pressurized, so the fuselage is pumped up like a balloon and relaxes again after landing (on the Comet the difference was about 8 psi, about 8 pounds pushing on every square inch). The skin carries that push around each window. Around a round corner the push spreads out; at a sharp corner it piles up in one tiny spot, several times higher than in the rest of the skin. Flight after flight the metal there is bent a little, and a fatigue crack slowly grows. To find the cause, engineers put a whole Comet (G-ALYU) in a water tank at Farnborough and pressurized it with water again and again (water stores little energy, so a failure would not explode). After about 3,060 pressure cycles (1,230 real flights plus 1,830 in the tank) the skin split open from the corner of a window-shaped escape-hatch cut-out. The cracks had started at rivet holes near the corners, where the stress was highest. Later Comets got rounded windows and thicker skin, and every airliner since has windows with rounded corners. The video simplifies a little: the Comet windows were squarish (small corner radii) rather than perfectly square.

Sources: FAA Lessons Learned, "de Havilland DH-106 Comet 1"; RAE/Cranfield report (Atkinson et al.), "Behaviour of skin fatigue cracks at the corners of windows in a Comet I fuselage"; Smithsonian Air & Space, "The Comet's Tale"; Wikipedia, "de Havilland Comet", "BOAC Flight 781", "South African Airways Flight 201".
