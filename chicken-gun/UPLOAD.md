## Description
Yes, this is real. Engineers load a chicken into a giant air cannon and fire it at a cockpit window at about 350 mph (around 35 psi of air pressure). Why? Birds hit planes all the time: in 2024 the FAA logged 22,372 wildlife strikes on U.S. aircraft, about 61 every day, and the real number is higher because reporting is voluntary. Most are harmless, but not all. On January 15, 2009, Canada geese knocked out both engines of US Airways Flight 1549 right after take-off from LaGuardia; the crew glided onto the Hudson River and all 155 people on board survived. Early tests showed how weak old windshields were: a 4-lb bird punched through one at just 75 mph. Today U.S. law (14 CFR 25.775) says the windshield panes in front of the pilots must stop a 4-pound bird at cruise speed without breaking through. So every new windshield is tested with a real bird. One rule: the chicken must not be frozen. A frozen bird hits like a block of ice, not like a real bird, so test birds are thawed first (the "frozen chicken" story is a famous legend).

Sources: FAA/USDA Wildlife Strike Report 1990–2024; NTSB report AAR-10/03 (US Airways Flight 1549); 14 CFR 25.775 (law.cornell.edu); ASTM F330 bird-impact test method; DVIDS, "Chickens first took flight at Arnold AFB 50 years ago"; Wikipedia, "Chicken gun"; Snopes, "Chicken gun".
