# Research: lightning is five times hotter than the Sun's surface

| Claim in the video | Basis |
|---|---|
| "This is five times hotter than the Sun." / "A lightning bolt heats the air to 50,000 degrees." | NWS: lightning can heat the air it passes through to 50,000 °F, five times hotter than the surface of the Sun (weather.gov/safety/lightning-temperature). NOAA SciJinks: up to 54,000 °F (30,000 °C). |
| "The surface of the Sun is about 10,000." | The Sun's photosphere is about 5,772 K ≈ 10,000 °F (NASA Sun fact sheet). The video says "the surface", not the core. |
| "The air heats up so fast, it explodes. That's thunder." | NSSL Severe Weather 101: the heat makes the air expand explosively; the shock wave becomes thunder. National Geographic says the same. |
| "And the bolt is only about an inch wide." | The current channel is a few centimetres wide, about 1 inch (HowStuffWorks: about an inch, 2–3 cm; WHAS: a few centimetres; the glow makes it look wider). |
| "It strikes about 44 times every second, somewhere on Earth." | NASA satellite lightning sensors (OTD/LIS): about 44 ± 5 flashes per second worldwide, ~1.4 billion per year (Christian et al. 2003, JGR). "Strikes" here means flashes, cloud-to-ground and in-cloud. |
| Loop: "So next time you see one, remember:" → "this is five times hotter than the Sun." | Grammatical sentence across the seam. |

Note: the comparison is about temperature, not energy. The video never claims a bolt has more energy than the Sun.
