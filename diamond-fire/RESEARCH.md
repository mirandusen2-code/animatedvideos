# Research: "This Diamond Is Burning Into Thin Air"

| Claim in the video | Value used | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| A diamond is pure carbon, like charcoal | diamond = crystalline carbon; charcoal = mostly carbon | Lavoisier's 1772 experiments (burning diamond gave only CO2); Live Science "Can diamonds burn?" |
| It burns at about 1,500 °F in pure oxygen | ignition ~690–840 °C (1,270–1,540 °F) in oxygen; ~900 °C (1,650 °F) in air | Science Focus "Is it possible to burn diamonds?" (700–900 °C); WTAMU Science Questions (Baird 2014); GIA / Gem Society "Can diamonds burn?" |
| Every atom leaves as carbon dioxide | C + O2 → CO2, an invisible gas (real gems leave traces of their impurities) | Live Science; Lavoisier |
| CO2 is the gas you breathe out | exhaled air ≈ 4 % CO2 | standard physiology |
| Diamonds slowly turn into graphite (pencil lead) | graphite is the stable form at room conditions (ΔG ≈ −2.9 kJ/mol); diamond is metastable | IUPAC Gold Book "diamond"; UW–Madison Chem 109 text |
| Too slowly to ever see | conversion blocked by a huge energy barrier; estimates ≥ 10^9 years at room temperature, so "1,000,000 years: still a diamond" is safe | arXiv 1409.0785 (graphite–diamond transition model); Chem 109 text |

Wording kept honest: "about 1,500 degrees" sits inside the published ignition range in oxygen; "pencil lead" is graphite (mixed with clay in real pencils); the time-lapse claims only that a diamond is unchanged after a million years.
