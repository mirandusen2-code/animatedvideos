# Research: how fast you are moving through space

| Claim in the video | Basis |
|---|---|
| "Earth spins you around at up to 1,000 mph" | Earth's rotation speed at the equator is about 1,040 mph (1,670 km/h); it is lower at higher latitudes ("up to"). NASA / Scientific American. |
| "it races around the Sun at 67,000" | Earth's mean orbital speed is about 29.8 km/s ≈ 67,000 mph (NASA Earth fact sheet). |
| "the Sun drags our whole solar system around the galaxy at 500,000" / "You're moving at half a million miles an hour" | The Sun orbits the Milky Way's centre at about 220–240 km/s ≈ 490,000–540,000 mph (NASA "Our Milky Way"; NASA Space Place quotes ~515,000 mph). The hook counts motion around the galaxy, the largest of these three everyday motions. |
| "One lap takes about 230 million years" | The galactic year is estimated at about 225–250 million years (NASA; Universe Today). |
| "Last time we were here, the first dinosaurs were just appearing" | One galactic year ago (~230 Myr) was the Late Triassic (Carnian); the earliest dinosaurs appear about 233–243 million years ago (Langer et al. 2010; Nesbitt 2011). Pangaea was still one supercontinent. |
| "And even when you're sitting perfectly still," → "you're moving at half a million miles an hour." | Loop sentence: all of the above happens regardless of what you are doing. |

Notes: speeds are given in mph (US channel). Speeds do not simply add (they point in different directions), so the video never sums them; the hook uses the galactic orbital speed on its own.

Visuals: NASA Blue Marble Next Generation texture (public domain, via the basemap-data package); Pangaea globe made by moving the present-day continents together (stylised, labelled "about 230 million years ago").
