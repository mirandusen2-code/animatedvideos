# How far is the Moon? Time the returning light.

| Narrated fact | Basis |
|---|---|
| Apollo astronauts left glass reflector arrays on the lunar surface. | [NASA, The Apollo Experiment That Keeps on Giving](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving/) and [NIST, To the Moon and Back in 2.5 Seconds](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds). “Mirror” is the hook. The next sentence specifies glass retroreflectors. |
| An Earth observatory emits a pulse. Only a small fraction of the light makes the round trip back to the station. | [NASA laser-ranging explanation](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/lro/laser-beams-reflected-between-earth-and-moon-boost-science/). The pulse drawn in space is a timing graphic. |
| The return is about 2.5 seconds later. | [NIST](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds) and [Williams et al., Journal of Geophysical Research](https://doi.org/10.1029/2021JE006920). Travel time varies with geometry. The diagram runs both legs in a total of 2.50 seconds. |
| Over long observation spans, lunar ranging shows the Moon receding at about 1.5 inches per year. | [NASA, Apollo ranging](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving/). This is a long-term trend; shorter-term orbital-distance changes are much larger. |
| Distance is speed of light times elapsed round-trip time divided by two in the elementary explanation. | [NIST](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds). High-precision analyses also model atmospheric delay and station and reflector motion. |
